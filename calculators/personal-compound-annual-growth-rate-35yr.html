Personal Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (35 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This personal version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 35 years. First, the growth ratio is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00. Using the CAGR formula, the rate is 2.00 to the power of one over 35, minus one, which is 2.00% a year. Check it back: $10,000 times 1.0200 to the power of 35 is about $20,000. Total growth is $10,000, or 100% over 35 years, yet the yearly rate is only 2.00%, because 35 years of compounding turns a tiny yearly rate into a doubling.

Who this term suits for a person: A 35-year term suits a person planning from early career to near retirement, for example a pension started at 25 and judged at 60, or a family fund spanning a generation. A 2.00% yearly rate is a reminder that over 35 years, regular contributions, career breaks, and inflation will shape the outcome far more than a single smooth growth rate.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this personal calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the rate drop only 0.34 points from 30 to 35 years?
Because the same doubling is spread over 5 extra years. The 30-year rate is 2.34% and the 35-year rate is 2.00%, a fall of 0.34 points, yet both describe the same $10,000 to $20,000 growth. As the term lengthens, each extra 5 years lowers the required rate by a smaller amount.

Is a 35-year CAGR reliable when careers and markets change?
Only as a backward-looking summary, not as a forecast. Over 35 years, jobs, tax rules, fund managers, and markets all change, and the single 2.00% figure hides every one of those changes. Use it to describe what happened, not to promise what the next 35 years will do.

Should contributions matter more than CAGR over 35 years?
Yes, for most people. A 2.00% CAGR on $10,000 gives $20,000 after 35 years, but adding even a small regular amount over the same period usually builds a far larger pot. Chasing a slightly higher CAGR matters less than saving regularly and avoiding long gaps.
