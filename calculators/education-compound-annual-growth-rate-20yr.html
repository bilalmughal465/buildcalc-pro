Education Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (20 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This education version uses a 20-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The CAGR result is the steady yearly rate that would take your starting value to your ending value over 20 years, if growth were perfectly smooth every year. It smooths out uneven years into a single yearly figure, so it describes the past window you entered rather than promising the same rate in the future.

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 20 years. First, the growth ratio is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00. Using the page formula, CAGR is 2.00 to the power of one over 20, minus one, which is 3.53% a year. That is the steady yearly rate that would take $10,000 to $20,000 in 20 years, if growth were perfectly smooth every year.

Doubling over 20 years needs only 3.53% a year. That sounds small, and it is: over two decades even a gentle yearly pace doubles money, which is why long education funds should be judged against fee rises, not against the doubling alone. Remember that CAGR smooths the path: the fund may have grown unevenly, with flat or negative years in between, and still show 3.53% a year. It describes the past window, it does not promise the next 20 years.

Who this term suits for an education saver: an education trust spanning a child's whole schooling, from early years to university over 20 years, where trustees want one yearly rate to report to the family instead of twenty separate yearly returns.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does doubling over 20 years need only 3.53% a year?
Because compounding over 20 years does most of the work. Small yearly gains, repeated for two decades, multiply to a doubling, so the yearly rate looks modest even though the total result is large.

Is 3.53% a year too low to plan a degree fund on?
It depends on fee inflation. If university costs rose faster than 3.53% a year, the fund doubled in cash but lost ground in real terms, so the rate must be read beside cost rises.

What breaks a 20-year CAGR estimate most often?
Assuming the past pace continues. Fees, returns, top-ups and withdrawals all change over 20 years, and a single smoothed rate cannot capture a change of course halfway through.
