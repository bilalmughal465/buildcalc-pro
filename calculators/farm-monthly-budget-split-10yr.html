Farm Monthly Budget Split Calculator (10 Years)

Split monthly income into needs, wants and savings. This farm version uses a 10-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Needs, Wants, and Savings figures split monthly income into essential farm and household costs, flexible spending, and money set aside, so the three amounts together should equal the monthly income entered.

Formula

Needs are 50 percent, wants are 30 percent and savings are 20 percent of income.

How it works

This 50-30-20 split is a planning guide, not a rule. Adjust it to your real costs.

Worked example

Monthly income $4,000, term 10 years. Using the page formula, needs are 50 percent of $4,000, which is $2,000 a month, wants are 30 percent, which is $1,200 a month, and savings are 20 percent, which is $800 a month. Those three shares add back to $4,000, which checks the split. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the same on every page in this group because the term is not an input.

As an illustration only, not the output of this page, keeping the same $4,000 every month for 10 years, which is 120 months, would total $480,000 of income, split into $240,000 for needs, $144,000 for wants and $96,000 for savings, before interest, fees, taxes or price rises. The illustration assumes income never varies, which is the weakest part of it for a farm: harvest income arrives in seasons, so a real farm must smooth good months into lean months for the monthly split to hold at all.

Who this term suits on a farm: an established farm working to a machinery cycle: tractors and major kit often need replacing about every ten years, so ten years of $800 a month, $96,000 in total before interest, is a realistic replacement fund to aim at, reviewed after every harvest.

FAQs

What is the 50-30-20 rule?
It is a simple budget guide: 50 percent needs, 30 percent wants and 20 percent savings.

Is this farm calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

What does $96,000 of savings over 10 years actually buy on a farm?
It is the sum of $800 a month over 120 months, before interest, fees or price rises. It is a realistic target for a machinery replacement fund, but a tractor that costs today's price will not cost the same price in ten years, so re-price the goal every year.

Why does the same $800 a month matter more over 10 years than over 5 years?
Because the habit survives more than one bad season. Five years can be one good run; ten years includes poor harvests, price falls and a machinery breakdown, so a split that still holds after ten years is a tested habit, not a lucky run.

Should the 50-30-20 split stay fixed for 10 years?
No. Review it after every season and after any big change, such as a new loan, a new tenancy or a change in herd size. A drought year may need 60 percent for needs, with wants cut and savings paused, and that is a better plan than pretending the old split still fits.
