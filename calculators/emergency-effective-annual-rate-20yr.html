Emergency Effective Annual Rate Calculator (20 Years)

Convert a nominal annual rate to an effective annual rate. This emergency version uses a 20-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Effective rate equals one plus nominal rate divided by periods, to the power of periods, minus one.

How it works

More frequent compounding raises the effective rate slightly above the nominal rate.

Worked example

Nominal rate 6%, compounding 12 times a year, term 20 years. The monthly factor is 1 plus 0.06 divided by 12, which is 1.005. Using the page formula, the effective annual rate is 1.005 to the power of 12, minus 1, which is 6.17% a year, not 6.00%. That extra 0.17% is the effect of interest earning interest within the year. Left alone, $10,000 in an emergency fund grows to $33,102.04 in 20 years at this monthly compounding, compared with $32,071.35 if interest were added only once a year at 6%, a difference of $1,030.69. For a 20-year emergency plan the gap has passed $1,000 on just $10,000, which is why a long emergency loan must be judged on its true rate, not on its headline.

Who this term suits for an emergency planner: a household planning a 20-year emergency buffer, for example a single earner covering mortgage and family costs, who should check the true rate before locking into a long emergency loan that may outlast the emergency itself.

FAQs

What is an effective rate?
The effective rate is the true yearly rate after allowing for compounding during the year.

Is this emergency calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the gap between EAR and nominal growth reach $1,030.69 at 20 years?
Because each year's extra interest itself earns interest in later years. The gap therefore grows faster than the years pass.

Is a 20-year emergency loan's EAR fixed for the whole term?
Only if the nominal rate, compounding frequency, fees and payments never change. In practice none of those is guaranteed for 20 years.

Should parents use EAR when choosing between a loan and saving?
Yes for the loan side, but compare like with like: a loan's EAR against a savings account's effective return, after fees and tax, not against either headline figure alone.
