Education Effective Annual Rate Calculator (35 Years)

Convert a nominal annual rate to an effective annual rate. This education version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Effective rate equals one plus nominal rate divided by periods, to the power of periods, minus one.

How it works

More frequent compounding raises the effective rate slightly above the nominal rate.

Worked example

Nominal rate 6%, compounding 12 times a year, term 35 years. The monthly factor is 1 plus 0.06 divided by 12, which is 1.005. Using the page formula, the effective annual rate is 1.005 to the power of 12, minus 1, which is 6.17% a year. Left alone, $10,000 in an education fund grows to $81,235.51 in 35 years at this monthly compounding, compared with $76,860.87 if interest were added only once a year at 6%, a difference of $4,374.64. The last 5 years alone add $21,009.76 to the fund, from $60,225.75 at year 30 to $81,235.51 at year 35, which shows how late years do most of the work in any long compounding plan.

Who this term suits for an education saver: a multi-generation education trust, for example a fund intended to help children and then grandchildren, where the compounding advantage is real but so is the risk, because fees, taxes, course costs and rates will all change many times over 35 years.

FAQs

What is an effective rate?
The effective rate is the true yearly rate after allowing for compounding during the year.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the last 5 years add so much at 35 years?
Because the fund is much larger by then. From $60,225.75 at year 30 to $81,235.51 at year 35 is $21,009.76 in 5 years, and that late growth is mostly interest on interest, not new saving.

Does EAR matter for a 35-year education trust?
Yes, and more than for a short fund. Over 35 years a small yearly difference becomes thousands of dollars, so choosing a trust or loan on its nominal rate alone can mislead by $4,374.64 on just $10,000.

What costs does EAR leave out over 35 years?
Fees, taxes, missed payments, gaps in saving, rate changes and rising education costs. None of these are in the 6.17% figure, and over 35 years they can matter more than the compounding frequency itself.
