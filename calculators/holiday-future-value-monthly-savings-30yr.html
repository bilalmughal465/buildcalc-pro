Holiday Future Value Monthly Savings Calculator (30 Years)

Estimate the future value of regular monthly savings. This holiday version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The future value is the total amount your monthly deposits plus the interest they earn would be worth at the end of the 30-year term, so it shows what your regular saving plan builds to by that date, not just the total you paid in.

Formula

Future value uses the monthly rate and the number of monthly deposits to grow each deposit with interest.

How it works

Each monthly deposit earns interest from the month it is made until the end of the term.

Worked example

Monthly deposit $500, annual rate 6%, term 30 years. The monthly rate is 6% divided by 12, which is 0.5%, and the number of deposits is 30 times 12, which is 360 months. Using the page formula, the future value is $500 times 1.005 to the power of 360, minus one, divided by 0.005. Since 1.005 to the power of 360 is about 6.02258, the multiplier is about 1004.5150, so the future value is $500 times 1004.5150, which is $502,257.52. That is the calculator's own output for these defaults.

You will have paid in $180,000 over the term, so interest adds $322,257.52, and the fund passes $500,000. The jump from 25 to 30 years alone adds about $155,760.54, more than the whole 15-year fund, which shows how fast compounding runs once the balance is large.

Who this term suits for a holiday saver: a saver building a retirement travel pot over about 30 years, intending to spend the fund gradually on holidays through retirement, rather than on one single trip at the end of the term.

FAQs

How does monthly saving grow?
Regular deposits grow because each deposit earns interest, and that interest then earns more interest.

Is this holiday calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the 30-year figure pass $500,000?
Because 360 deposits of $500 total $180,000, and at 6% a year compounded monthly they earn $322,257.52 of interest, giving $502,257.52 in total. Once the balance is large, each year's interest alone is bigger than a whole year of deposits.

Can a $500,000 holiday fund still buy the same travel in 30 years?
Not necessarily. $502,257.52 is a nominal figure. If travel, hotel and flight prices rise over 30 years, the same journeys will cost far more than they do today, so the fund's buying power will be much smaller than its headline number suggests.

What should a holiday saver do if the fund is needed early?
Take only what the trip needs and leave the rest compounding. Withdrawing the whole fund early ends the compounding that makes the later years so large, so an early withdrawal costs far more than the amount taken out.
