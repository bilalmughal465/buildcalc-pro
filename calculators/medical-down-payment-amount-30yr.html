Medical Down Payment Amount Calculator (30 Years)

Calculate a down payment from price and percentage. This medical version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Down payment figure is the upfront cash amount to pay at purchase, and the Loan needed figure is the remaining amount to borrow after that upfront payment, so a larger down payment means a smaller loan to repay.

Formula

Down payment equals price times down payment percentage divided by 100.

How it works

A larger down payment usually means a smaller loan and lower monthly payments.

Worked example

Price $300,000, down payment percentage 20 percent, term 30 years. Using the page formula, down payment equals $300,000 times 20 divided by 100, which is $60,000. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults. The loan needed is $300,000 minus $60,000, which is $240,000. That headline is the same on every page in this group, because the term is not an input on this page: only the price and the percentage decide the answer.

As a labelled illustration only, not the output of this page, saving steadily toward that deposit would mean about $166.67 a month for 360 months, since $166.67 times 360 is about $60,000. That illustration leaves out interest earned on savings, fees, taxes and price changes, so it is a planning habit, not a promise.

Who this term suits for a medical borrower: a long-horizon fund for a future clinic freehold, where $166.67 a month is the cheapest monthly pace of all, but where the real risk is that $300,000 will no longer buy the same premises in 30 years.

FAQs

How much down payment do I need?
It depends on the lender and the purchase. A common planning figure is 20 percent, but requirements vary.

Is this medical calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is a 30-year deposit plan the same as a clinic mortgage?
No. The deposit is only the first $60,000. The $240,000 loan that follows is a separate, long commitment, and its monthly cost matters more than the deposit pace.

What happens if practice income falls for several years in a 30-year plan?
Then the $166.67 pace is easy to keep, but the eventual loan may no longer be affordable. Income, not the deposit, is the binding constraint over 30 years.

Should a practice borrow up to the full $300,000 price after 30 years?
Only if the loan payment fits the practice income at that time. A deposit saved long ago does not guarantee that a large loan is wise.
