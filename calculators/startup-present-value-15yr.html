Startup Present Value Calculator (15 Years)

Find how much a future amount is worth today. This startup version uses a 15-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Present value is what a future amount is worth in today’s money, so a figure well below the future amount shows how much value waiting 15 years removes.

Formula

Present value equals future amount divided by one plus rate to the power of years.

How it works

A future amount is worth less today because money today can earn interest.

Worked example

Future amount $50,000, annual rate 5 percent, term 15 years. Using the page formula, present value equals $50,000 divided by 1.05 to the power of 15, which is $24,050.85. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults. The discount for waiting is $50,000 minus $24,050.85, which is $25,949.15.

Who this term suits for a startup founder: a founder valuing a long-horizon startup asset, such as a patent payout, a product line that matures in about 15 years, or a deferred exit payment, where waiting more than halves the value.

FAQs

What is present value?
Present value is what a future sum is worth today after allowing for interest over time.

Is this startup calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why is $50,000 in 15 years worth less than half its face value today?
Because money halves in about 14.21 years at 5 percent, so by 15 years the divisor 1.05 to the power of 15 is about 2.079, giving $24,050.85. The discount of $25,949.15 is more than half the future sum.

Should a startup plan 15 years ahead on one fixed 5 percent rate?
No. Over 15 years rates, markets, and the startup itself will change, so treat $24,050.85 as a planning estimate and test other rates: at 7 percent the same sum is worth only $18,122.30 today.

Does a 15-year present value help a founder choose between two offers?
Yes. If one buyer pays $24,000 now and another promises $50,000 in 15 years, the two are nearly equal at 5 percent, so the founder should weigh risk and timing, not just the headline amount.
