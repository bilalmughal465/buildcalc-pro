Retirement Present Value Calculator (20 Years)

Find how much a future amount is worth today. This retirement version uses a 20-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Present value figure is the lump sum needed today that would grow to the future amount entered, at the rate and over the 20-year term entered, so a smaller present value means time and interest are doing more of the work, while a larger one means more must be set aside today.

Formula

Present value equals future amount divided by one plus rate to the power of years.

How it works

A future amount is worth less today because money today can earn interest.

Worked example

Future amount $50,000, annual rate 5%, term 20 years. Using the page formula, present value equals $50,000 divided by 1.05 to the power of 20, which is $18,844.47. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults. The discount is $50,000 minus $18,844.47, which is $31,155.53, so time and interest remove $31,155.53 of value between today and year 20.

Who this term suits for a retirement saver: a saver in their forties with about 20 years left before retirement, who is building a retirement fund steadily and wants to see how much a distant future cost is really worth today.

FAQs

What is present value?
Present value is what a future sum is worth today after allowing for interest over time.

Is this retirement calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why is $50,000 in 20 years worth less than $19,000 today?
Because 20 years of compounding at 5% a year more than doubles money, so only $18,844.47 today is needed to reach $50,000 in 20 years; the rest is growth, not saving.

Should a 20-year retirement plan be judged on present value or future value?
Both: present value tells you what to set aside today, while future value tells you what the fund will be worth at retirement; comparing the two shows how much of the plan is growth and how much is your own saving.

What happens to this figure if rates rise to 7%?
The present value falls further, because a higher rate grows today's money faster, so less is needed now to reach $50,000 in 20 years; at a lower rate, more would be needed.
