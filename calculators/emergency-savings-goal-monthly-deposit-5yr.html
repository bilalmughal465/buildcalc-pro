Emergency Savings Goal Monthly Deposit Calculator (5 Years)

Find the monthly deposit needed to reach a savings goal. This emergency version uses a 5-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Monthly deposit equals goal times monthly rate divided by one plus monthly rate to the power of months minus one.

How it works

Saving a fixed amount each month with interest reaches the goal faster than saving without interest.

Worked example

A household wants a $50,000 emergency fund in 5 years, at 5% a year. The monthly rate is 5% divided by 12, which is 0.0041667, and there are 60 monthly deposits. Using the page formula, the monthly deposit is $50,000 times 0.0041667, divided by 1.0041667 to the power of 60 minus 1, which is $735.23 a month. Over 60 deposits the household pays in $44,113.70, and interest adds $5,886.30, which is 11.8% of the goal.

After only 5 years most of the goal still comes from the saver's own deposits, because interest has had little time to work. For an emergency fund this is the most practical horizon in this group: five years of steady saving builds a fund that can cover a job loss, a major medical bill or a home repair without borrowing at card rates, and the money should stay in an account that can be reached at once, not locked away.

Who this term suits for an emergency saver: a single earner who has just started a steady job and wants a real safety net within five years, for example a worker saving toward twelve months of essential bills so that one redundancy or illness does not force a loan.

FAQs

How much should I save each month?
Divide the goal by the growth of regular deposits at the assumed rate to get the monthly amount.

Is this emergency calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is $735.23 a month realistic for an emergency fund?
It is a large monthly amount, and it only fits a household with room in its budget after rent, food and transport. If $735.23 is out of reach, a smaller first target, such as three months of essential bills, is a better start than no fund at all, and this calculator can be re-run with that smaller goal.

Why is interest only $5,886.30 after 5 years?
Because most of the $50,000 is paid in by the saver, and interest has only five years to compound. The fund is still worth building: the protection comes from having $50,000 available, not from the $5,886.30 of interest.

Should an emergency fund be invested for a higher return?
No, not the part meant for emergencies. The first fund should stay in instant-access cash, even at a lower return, because an emergency fund that cannot be reached on the day it is needed has failed its job, whatever its headline rate.
