Startup Compound Amount Calculator (10 Years)

Calculate the amount after compound interest over time. This startup version uses a 10-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Amount is the full value at the end of the term, including the original principal, and the Interest is the growth earned on top of that principal, so the Amount is the total sum available at the end.

Formula

Amount equals principal times one plus rate to the power of years.

How it works

Interest is added to the balance each year, so later interest is charged on a larger amount.

Worked example

Principal $10,000, annual rate 6 percent, term 10 years. Using the page formula, the amount is $10,000 times 1.06 to the power of 10, which gives $17,908.48. That is the calculator output for these defaults. The interest earned is $17,908.48 minus $10,000, which is $7,908.48.

That is growth of about 79 percent on the original $10,000. Notice the second 5 years add $4,526.22 of interest, more than the $3,382.26 added in the first 5 years, because interest is now earned on a larger balance.

Who this term suits for a startup founder: A founder saving for a medium-term goal, such as replacing core equipment, fitting out a first proper office, or building a product line that needs about 10 years of steady funding.

FAQs

What is compound interest?
Compound interest is interest earned on both the principal and on interest already added.

Is this startup calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

What does $10,000 become after 10 years at 6% for a startup fund?
Using the page formula, $10,000 times 1.06 to the power of 10 gives $17,908.48, so the interest is $7,908.48. That is the calculator output for these defaults.

Why is 10-year interest more than double the 5-year interest?
Because compounding builds on itself: the first 5 years add $3,382.26, but the second 5 years add $4,526.22, since interest is then earned on $13,382.26 rather than on $10,000.

What if the rate were 8% instead of 6% over 10 years?
At 8 percent, $10,000 times 1.08 to the power of 10 gives $21,589.25, so the interest would be $11,589.25. A higher rate helps a lot over 10 years, but it is not guaranteed.
