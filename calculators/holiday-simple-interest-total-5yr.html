Holiday Simple Interest Total Calculator (5 Years)

Calculate simple interest and the total amount to repay. This holiday version uses a 5-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Simple interest equals principal times rate times time, divided by 100.

How it works

Interest is charged only on the original principal, not on interest already added.

Worked example

Principal $10,000, annual rate 5%, term 5 years. Using the page formula, simple interest is $10,000 times 5 times 5, divided by 100, which is $2,500. The total amount is $10,000 plus $2,500, which is $12,500. That is the calculator's own output for these defaults.

That is $500 of interest a year, every year, because simple interest never compounds: year 1 adds $500, and year 5 adds the same $500. For a holiday fund this is a short, clear plan: five years of steady saving can turn a $10,000 starting sum into $12,500, but only if the holiday price itself does not rise faster than the interest earned.

Who this term suits for a holiday saver: A family saving for one big holiday in about 5 years, for example a first overseas trip or a honeymoon put off for a few seasons, who want to know exactly what a fixed sum will be worth before they start looking at flights and hotels.

FAQs

What is simple interest?
Simple interest is interest charged only on the original amount, not on accumulated interest.

Is this holiday calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is $2,500 of interest a lot for a 5-year holiday fund?
It is a quarter of the starting sum, which is fair for 5 years at 5% simple interest, but it only helps if holiday prices rise by less than $2,500 over the same 5 years. If flights and hotels rise faster, the extra $2,500 may simply cover inflation, not buy a better holiday.

Should I save for a holiday over 5 years, or book sooner and borrow less?
Saving first is usually safer, because borrowing for a holiday adds interest you must repay after the trip is over. A 5-year saving plan suits a holiday that can wait; if the trip cannot wait, a smaller, nearer holiday may be wiser than a large loan for a distant one.

What if holiday prices rise faster than 5% a year over 5 years?
Then $12,500 may buy less holiday than $10,000 buys today, even though the number is larger. Check the price of the actual trip you want, not just the total on this page, and review the plan each year against real flight and hotel prices.
