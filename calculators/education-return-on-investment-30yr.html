Education Return on Investment Calculator (30 Years)

Calculate return on investment as a percentage. This education version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Return equals final value minus invested amount, divided by invested amount, times 100.

How it works

A positive return means the final value is higher than the amount invested.

Worked example

Amount invested $10,000, final value $15,000, term 30 years. First, the gain is $15,000 minus $10,000, which is $5,000. Using the page formula, return is $5,000 divided by $10,000, times 100, which is 50.00%. That is the total return over the whole 30 years, not a yearly rate. As an illustration only, not the output of this page, the annualised rate behind the same result is about 1.36% a year, because $15,000 divided by $10,000 is 1.50, and the 30th root of 1.50 is about 1.0136. Over 30 years the yearly pace is about 1.36%. For education planning this is a generational check: 50.00% over 30 years means the money barely grew each year, and almost certainly fell behind fee rises. All figures here are nominal, before fees, taxes, course upkeep and lost earnings while studying, which this page does not count, and they are planning estimates, not promises.

Who this term suits for an education learner: a family or trust looking back 30 years at money put into education, for example a grandparent's fund for a child's schooling that is now being judged on its result.

FAQs

What is a good ROI?
There is no single good figure. Compare returns over the same period and with the same risk before deciding.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is $5,000 gain on $10,000 over 30 years a good result for education?
No, not on its own. Spread over 30 years it is about 1.36% a year as an illustration only, which is a weak pace against three decades of rising education costs.

Why does 1.36% a year matter more than the 50.00% headline at 30 years?
Because the headline hides the time taken. A total return without its window flatters long, slow results and punishes fast ones, so the yearly pace is the fair comparison.

What would make a 30-year education ROI misleading?
Counting only the first course's cost while ignoring later fees, counting a career's whole earnings as the course's value, or assuming fees stayed at their level 30 years ago.
