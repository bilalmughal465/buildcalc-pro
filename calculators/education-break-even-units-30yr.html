Education Break Even Units Calculator (30 Years)

Find how many units must be sold to break even. This education version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Break even units equal fixed costs divided by price minus variable cost per unit.

How it works

At break even, total revenue exactly covers total costs, with no profit and no loss.

Worked example

Fixed costs $10,000, price per unit $50, variable cost per unit $30, term 30 years. First, the contribution per unit is $50 minus $30, which is $20. Using the page formula, break even units are $10,000 divided by $20, which is 500 units. At 500 units, revenue is 500 times $50, which is $25,000, and total cost is $10,000 of fixed costs plus 500 times $30 of variable costs, which is $10,000 plus $15,000, also $25,000, so profit is zero. The term in years does not change this answer, because the formula uses only fixed costs, price and variable cost. As a pacing illustration only, not the output of this page, spreading 500 units evenly over 30 years is about 16.67 units a year, or about 1.39 units a month. Over 30 years, a course, a price and a cost base will all change beyond recognition, so this page is a maths check, not a 30-year business plan.

Who this term suits for an education provider: a long-running education trust or adult-learning centre, for example a centre checking that a $10,000 legacy project, such as a library collection or a scholarship fund launch, is covered by 500 memberships or books, spread in illustration over 30 years.

FAQs

What is break even?
Break even is the point where revenue covers all costs, so profit is zero.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is about 16.67 units a year a plan for 30 years?
No, it is only 500 divided by 30. No centre should plan 30 years ahead on a single price of $50 and a single variable cost of $30; both will change many times, and each year's break even must be recalculated with that year's real numbers.

Why does the 30-year page give the same 500 units as the 5-year page?
Because the formula has no year input at all. Fixed costs, price and variable cost are the whole calculation, so identical inputs give identical break even on every page in the group.

What would make the 30-year figure misleading?
Treating the $10,000 as the only fixed cost over 30 years. In reality, a centre pays fixed costs every year, so its true 30-year fixed-cost total is roughly 30 times its yearly fixed costs, and its true break even is far above 500 units.
