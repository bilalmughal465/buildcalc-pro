Education Future Value Monthly Savings Calculator (15 Years)

Estimate the future value of regular monthly savings. This education version uses a 15-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Future value uses the monthly rate and the number of monthly deposits to grow each deposit with interest.

How it works

Each monthly deposit earns interest from the month it is made until the end of the term.

Worked example

A family saves $500 every month for 15 years at 6% a year for a child's education. The monthly rate is 6% divided by 12, which is 0.005, and there are 180 monthly deposits. Using the page formula, the future value is $500 times ((1.005 to the power of 180 minus 1) divided by 0.005), which is $145,409.36. The family paid in $90,000, so interest added $55,409.36, which is 38.1% of the future value. Interest now passes half the amount the family paid in, and the last 5 years alone added $63,469.69 to the fund.

Who this term suits for an education saver: a family whose child is still in primary school and will reach university in about 15 years, for example parents who want one fund to cover a degree and possibly a postgraduate year, and who can review the plan when the child chooses subjects and a country for study.

FAQs

How does monthly saving grow?
Regular deposits grow because each deposit earns interest, and that interest then earns more interest.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

How does a 15-year term balance the monthly amount and the interest?
Fifteen years is long enough for interest to matter, at $55,409.36, while the monthly deposit stays at $500. Shorter terms need a much larger monthly payment for the same goal, and longer terms lean even harder on a fixed rate holding for decades.

What if the rate falls below 6% over 15 years?
The fund falls below $145,409.36, and the shortfall grows with every year the lower rate applies. A family should recheck the plan whenever the rate changes, rather than trusting a single 15-year figure made today.

Can a 15-year fund cover a second degree or postgraduate study?
It can help, but only if the first degree does not use it all. Ring-fencing part of the fund for later study, instead of spending the whole pot on the first degree, is what makes a second stage possible.
