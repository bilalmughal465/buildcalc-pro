Wedding Monthly Budget Split Calculator (35 Years)

Split monthly income into needs, wants and savings. This wedding version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Needs are 50 percent, wants are 30 percent and savings are 20 percent of income.

How it works

This 50-30-20 split is a planning guide, not a rule. Adjust it to your real costs.

Worked example

Monthly income $4,000, term 35 years. Needs are 50 percent of $4,000, which is $2,000 a month. Wants are 30 percent, which is $1,200 a month. Savings are 20 percent, which is $800 a month. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the same on every page in this group because the term is not an input: only the monthly income is entered. As an illustration only, keeping the same $800 a month of savings for the full 35-year window (420 months) would add up to $336,000, before any interest earned and before any change in income; the page itself does not output that total.

Who this term suits for a wedding: a very long household savings habit, where no wedding should be planned on one unchanged split for thirty-five years, and the figure is useful only as a reminder of what a steady habit adds up to.

FAQs

What is the 50-30-20 rule?
It is a simple budget guide: 50 percent needs, 30 percent wants and 20 percent savings.

Is this wedding calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the total matter more than the $800 headline at 35 years?
Because $800 a month sounds small, but over 420 months it adds up to $336,000 before interest, and that total is what a long habit really means. The headline is the monthly habit; the total is the consequence of keeping it for thirty-five years.

Who keeps a 35-year wedding budget if the couple's circumstances change?
The couple themselves, by reviewing it. Over thirty-five years there will be job changes, children, moves and possibly a different wedding altogether, so the split must be handed a fresh review whenever life changes, or it stops describing real spending.

Is 35 years too long to judge from one budget split?
Yes, as a single fixed plan. A 35-year figure is useful only as an illustration of what a steady habit adds up to; no wedding, income or price level should be trusted unchanged for thirty-five years.
