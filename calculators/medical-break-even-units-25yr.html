Medical Break Even Units Calculator (25 Years)

Find how many units must be sold to break even. This medical version uses a 25-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Break even units figure is the number of units that must be sold to cover all costs exactly, with no profit and no loss, so every unit sold beyond that figure starts to add profit.

Formula

Break even units equal fixed costs divided by price minus variable cost per unit.

How it works

At break even, total revenue exactly covers total costs, with no profit and no loss.

Worked example

Fixed costs $10,000, price per unit $50, variable cost per unit $30, term 25 years. First, the contribution per unit is $50 minus $30, which is $20. Using the page formula, break even units are $10,000 divided by $20, which is 500.00 units. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the same on every page in this group because the term is not an input. At break even, revenue is 500 times $50, which is $25,000, variable costs are 500 times $30, which is $15,000, and $25,000 minus $15,000 minus $10,000 of fixed costs leaves zero profit, exactly as break even should.

Spreading those 500 units evenly over 25 years is about 20.00 units a year. That spread is a planning average only, not the output of this page: the calculator does not spread sales over time, and real patient numbers, prices and costs will change year to year.

Who this term suits for a medical practice: an established dental or physiotherapy practice planning a partner buy-in, a practice succession, or a long lease renewal, where the next quarter-century of patient income must justify today's fixed costs.

FAQs

What is break even?
Break even is the point where revenue covers all costs, so profit is zero.

Is this medical calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Does a 25-year break even suit a dental or physiotherapy practice?
It can, for long decisions such as buying premises or taking on a partner, but it should never be the only number used, because treatment mix and fees change far faster than 25 years.

What if price falls to $40 for part of 25 years?
Then the contribution per unit falls to $10 for those sales, and break even for the affected part rises sharply, so the practice should model the lower price separately rather than trusting the $50 example.

How often should a 25-year medical break even be recalculated?
At least once a year, and whenever rent, wages, fees or supply costs change, because a 25-year figure built on today's numbers goes stale quickly.
