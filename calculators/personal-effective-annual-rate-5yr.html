Personal Effective Annual Rate Calculator (5 Years)

Convert a nominal annual rate to an effective annual rate. This personal version uses a 5-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Effective rate equals one plus nominal rate divided by periods, to the power of periods, minus one.

How it works

More frequent compounding raises the effective rate slightly above the nominal rate.

Worked example

Nominal rate 6%, compounded monthly (12 periods per year), term 5 years. First, the effective annual rate is (1 + 0.06 / 12) to the power of 12, minus one, which is 6.17%. That is the calculator's own formula: the effective rate is slightly above the 6% nominal rate because interest compounds each month. On $10,000, the effective rate gives $13,488.50 after 5 years, a gain of $3,488.50. At a flat 6% with no monthly compounding, the same $10,000 would give $13,382.26, so monthly compounding adds $106.25 over 5 years. The effective rate itself does not change with the term; what changes is how long that 6.17% has to compound, which is why the 5-year total is so much larger than a short-term total.

Who this term suits for a person: A 5-year term suits a person saving for a near, fixed goal who wants to compare bank accounts honestly, for example topping up a house deposit, building a wedding fund, or replacing a car in five years. The monthly-compounded account wins by only $106.25 over five years on $10,000, so fees, access, and whether the rate is fixed matter almost as much as the headline rate.

FAQs

What is an effective rate?
The effective rate is the true yearly rate after allowing for compounding during the year.

Is this personal calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why is the effective rate 6.17% when the bank says 6%?
Because the bank quotes a nominal rate before compounding. With monthly compounding, each month's interest also earns interest, so (1 + 0.06 / 12) to the power of 12, minus one, gives 6.17%. The 0.17 percentage point gap is the compounding effect, not an extra charge.

Is $10,000 growing to $13,488.50 in 5 years realistic?
It is arithmetic, not a promise. $10,000 at an effective rate of 6.17% for 5 years gives $13,488.50, a gain of $3,488.50, if the rate never changes and no fees or tax apply. A real account with fees, a variable rate, or withdrawals will give a different result, so treat the figure as a comparison, not a guarantee.

Should I pick monthly compounding over annual compounding for a 5-year goal?
For a 5-year goal, monthly compounding at 6% nominal gives $13,488.50, while annual compounding at 6% gives $13,382.26, a difference of only $106.25 on $10,000. Pick the account with the higher effective rate after fees, but do not ignore access, minimum balances, and whether the rate is fixed.
