Farm Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (5 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This farm version uses a 5-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The CAGR result is the steady yearly growth rate that would turn the starting value into the ending value over 5 years, so a higher percentage means the farm value grew faster on average across that period.

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 5 years. First, the growth factor is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00, so the value doubled. Using the page formula, CAGR is 2.00 to the power of one over 5, minus one, times 100. The 5th root of 2.00 is about 1.1487, so CAGR is about 14.87% a year. That is the calculator's own output for the page defaults. It is a smoothed average, not a claim that the farm grew by 14.87% in every single year: on a real farm some years grow fast and some stand still or fall back, and CAGR averages them into one steady rate.

Who this term suits on a farm: a farm that has doubled a small asset in a short burst, such as a young orchard block, a small dairy herd, or a new vegetable enterprise in about 5 years, checking whether the growth is steady or just one or two bumper seasons.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this farm calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is 14.87% a year over 5 years realistic for a farm?
It is the steady rate that doubles $10,000 to $20,000 in 5 years, but on a real farm it rarely arrives evenly. One bumper harvest or a sharp price rise can lift the total, so check the year-by-year figures before treating 14.87% as a normal yearly return.

Why does the same doubling look so strong over only 5 years?
Because the whole gain is squeezed into a short window. Doubling in 5 years needs about 14.87% a year, while the same doubling over 40 years needs only about 1.75% a year, so a short term always makes the yearly rate look larger.

Should a farmer plan the next 5 years on 14.87% a year?
No, not on this figure alone. Use it as a backward-looking average for the doubling that already happened, then plan forward from expected yields, prices, and costs, not from a past average.
