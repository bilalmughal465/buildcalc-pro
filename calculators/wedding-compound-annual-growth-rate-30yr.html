Wedding Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (30 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This wedding version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

In plain terms, this percentage is the steady yearly growth rate that would take the starting value to the ending value over 30 years, smoothing out any uneven growth along the way.

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 30 years. The ending value divided by the starting value is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2. Using the page formula, the compound annual growth rate is 2 to the power of one over 30, minus one, which gives 2.34 percent a year. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the same starting and ending values as every page in this group, because the term is the only input that changes: a longer term spreads the same doubling over more years, so the yearly rate falls as the term rises.

Who this term suits for a wedding: a lifetime wedding fund, such as saving from a young age toward a wedding decades later, where the yearly rate looks tiny but the doubling is real.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this wedding calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

What does a 2.34 percent rate over 30 years mean for a wedding fund?
It means patience replaces a high return. Two to the power of one over 30, minus one, is 2.34 percent a year, which still doubles $10,000 to $20,000, but only because the money compounds for three decades.

Is a 30-year wedding figure a real budget?
Only as a rough guide. No couple can know wedding prices 30 years ahead, so the $20,000 target and the 2.34 percent rate are planning figures, useful for starting a fund, not a fixed price for a wedding.

What if the return is only 2 percent over 30 years?
Then $10,000 grows to about $18,113.62 in 30 years, which is $1,886.38 short of $20,000. Even a small gap in the yearly rate leaves a visible shortfall over 30 years, so the fund needs a top-up or a longer term.
