Car Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (35 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This car version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 35 years. The ratio is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00. Raise 2.00 to the power of one over 35, subtract one, and multiply by 100: the compound annual growth rate is 2.00%. That means the car value would need to grow by about 2.00% every single year, steadily, to double in 35 years. After one year at that steady rate, the value would be about $10,200. These figures are nominal, before inflation, fees, tax, insurance, servicing, and repair costs, and they assume steady growth every year, which real car values almost never do. Most everyday cars lose value over time, so a doubling like this fits a sought-after classic or a rising used-car market, not a normal family car.

Who this term suits for a car: an owner of a rare model checking, after 35 years, whether slow yearly growth still beats what was spent on storage, repairs, and insurance, before committing to an auction or another decade of ownership.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this car calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the yearly rate fall to 2.00% at 35 years even though total growth is unchanged?
Because the same $10,000 gain is now spread over 35 years instead of 5. The maths is fixed: doubling over a longer period always means a lower yearly rate, which is why a long-held car can double in price yet still be a poor yearly return.

How do long gaps in use affect a 35-year car CAGR?
Cars left unused still cost money through storage, insurance, and decay, and they often need major work before they can be driven or sold. Those costs do not appear in CAGR, so a 35-year figure can look steady while the real cost of ownership kept rising.

Should a 35-year car CAGR be compared with a shorter, lower-risk return?
Yes. Over 35 years, even a modest savings return compounded yearly would usually beat 2.00% a year with far less risk and no repair bills. A car should be kept for enjoyment or use first, and judged as an investment only after those costs are counted.
