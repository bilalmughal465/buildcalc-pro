Car Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (40 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This car version uses a 40-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 40 years. The ratio is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00. Raise 2.00 to the power of one over 40, subtract one, and multiply by 100: the compound annual growth rate is 1.75%. That means the car value would need to grow by about 1.75% every single year, steadily, to double in 40 years. After one year at that steady rate, the value would be about $10,175. These figures are nominal, before inflation, fees, tax, insurance, servicing, and repair costs, and they assume steady growth every year, which real car values almost never do. Most everyday cars lose value over time, so a doubling like this fits a sought-after classic or a rising used-car market, not a normal family car.

Who this term suits for a car: nobody looking for a success story: a car that only doubles in 40 years is a warning check, because storage, insurance, servicing, and restoration over a working lifetime almost certainly cost more than the gain, so the figure is useful only to show why most cars should never be bought as investments.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this car calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is doubling over 40 years actually a poor result for a car?
Yes, as a yearly return it is poor: only 1.75% a year. A car that takes a full working lifetime to double has barely grown, and 40 years of insurance, servicing, storage, and repairs will almost always exceed the $10,000 gain.

What does 1.75% a year mean for buying power over 40 years?
If prices rose faster than 1.75% a year on average, the final $20,000 buys less than the original $10,000 did. Over 40 years that is very likely, so the owner may have waited a lifetime to end up able to buy less than at the start.

What should an owner check before trusting a 40-year car CAGR figure?
The full ownership bill, the car's real condition and saleability today, and whether the same money kept elsewhere would have done better. A 40-year CAGR is a warning check for a lifetime of car ownership, not a reason to buy a car as an investment.
