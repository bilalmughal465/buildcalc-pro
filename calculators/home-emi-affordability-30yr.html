Home EMI Affordability Calculator (30 Years)

Estimate a safe monthly EMI from income. This home version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Safe EMI equals monthly income times safe share divided by 100.

How it works

Lenders often use a share of income as a guide, but other debts also matter.

Worked example

Monthly home income $5,000, safe EMI share 30 percent, term 30 years. Using the page formula, safe EMI equals $5,000 times 30 divided by 100, which is $1,500 per month. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the same on every page in this group because the term is not an input.

As an illustration only, not the output of this page, paying $1,500 every month for 30 years would total $540,000 in monthly payments. That total is not the loan amount you can borrow, because this page does not include interest, fees, or a changing rate, and home income will not stay at $5,000 every month for 30 years.

Who this term suits for a home buyer: a household taking the most common long mortgage term, where the monthly payment feels manageable today but the commitment runs for most of a working life, so the plan must survive pay rises, pay cuts, and family changes.

FAQs

How much EMI can I afford?
A common guide is 30 percent of monthly income, but total debts and living costs must also fit.

Is this home calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is a 30-year home EMI the same as a house mortgage?
Not exactly. This page gives a safe monthly figure from income alone, while a real mortgage also depends on the house price, the deposit, the interest rate, and the lender's checks. The $1,500 here is a planning guide, not a mortgage offer.

What happens if household income falls for several years in a 30-year plan?
The EMI stays the same while income falls, so the household has less left for food, utilities, and repairs. A 30-year plan should therefore include savings for a fall in income, and the household should talk to the lender early if payments become difficult, rather than waiting.

Should I borrow up to the full $1,500 for 30 years?
Only if the household can still save and maintain the home after paying it. Borrowing to the ceiling leaves no room for a broken boiler, a job gap, or rising bills, and over 30 years at least one of those is likely to happen.
