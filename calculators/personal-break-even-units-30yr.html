Personal Break Even Units Calculator (30 Years)

Find how many units must be sold to break even. This personal version uses a 30-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Break even units equal fixed costs divided by price minus variable cost per unit.

How it works

At break even, total revenue exactly covers total costs, with no profit and no loss.

Worked example

Fixed costs $10,000, price per unit $50, variable cost per unit $30, term 30 years. First, the contribution per unit is $50 minus $30, which is $20. Break even units are $10,000 divided by $20, which is 500 units. At 500 units, revenue is 500 times $50, which is $25,000, variable costs are 500 times $30, which is $15,000, and total costs are $15,000 plus $10,000, which is $25,000, so revenue exactly covers costs. As a planning pace only, not as an output of this calculator, reaching 500 units over 30 years means 16.67 a year, which is 1.39 a month. Selling 100 units more, 600 units in total, gives revenue of $30,000 against total costs of $28,000, so the profit is $2,000, because each extra unit adds $20.

Who this term suits for a person: A 30-year term suits a lifelong hobby that also brings in a little money, for example someone making quilts, pottery, or preserves for most of their working life and selling a few pieces each year. The pace is 16.67 units a year, so pressure is very low, but a 30-year plan must be reviewed often, because no hobby, price, or sales channel stays the same for three decades.

FAQs

What is break even?
Break even is the point where revenue covers all costs, so profit is zero.

Is this personal calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is a 30-year term normal for a personal break-even plan?
For a business with a shop and staff, 30 years is a long horizon, and for a personal hobby it is even longer. The 500-unit answer is still correct on the numbers given, but it is only useful if the price of $50, the variable cost of $30, and the fixed costs of $10,000 are reviewed regularly. Treat 30 years as a lifetime hobby frame, not as a sales forecast.

How can the risk of a 30-year plan be cut?
Break the plan into 5-year checks. After each 5-year block, compare units sold with the pace of 16.67 units a year, update the price and variable cost, and reset the remaining target. If costs have risen, re-run the calculator with the new figures instead of carrying the old 500-unit figure forward unchanged.

What is a person trading when they choose 30 years over 5 years?
Pressure for patience. Over 5 years the pace is 100 units a year, which forces quick selling but clears the fixed costs fast. Over 30 years the pace is only 16.67 units a year, which is easy to meet in a good year, but the fixed costs stay unpaid for far longer, and the product may be out of date long before 500 units are sold.
