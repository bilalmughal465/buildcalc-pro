Holiday Return on Investment Calculator (35 Years)

Calculate return on investment as a percentage. This holiday version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Return percentage shows the total gain on the amount invested over the full 35 years, so a positive figure means the final value is higher than the amount invested, while a negative figure would mean money was lost; it is a total for the whole period, not a yearly rate.

Formula

Return equals final value minus invested amount, divided by invested amount, times 100.

How it works

A positive return means the final value is higher than the amount invested.

Worked example

Amount invested $10,000, final value $15,000, term 35 years. First, the gain is $15,000 minus $10,000, which is $5,000. Using the page formula, return is $5,000 divided by $10,000, times 100, which is 50.00%. That is the total return over the whole 35 years, not a yearly rate.

As an illustration only, not the output of this page, the annualised rate behind the same result is about 1.17% a year, because $15,000 divided by $10,000 is 1.50, and the 35th root of 1.50 is about 1.0117. Over 35 years the yearly pace is close to 1%, so the fund has barely grown in real holiday terms. Long gaps without a holiday do not help the reading: they only show how long the money sat while travel prices kept moving.

Who this term suits for a holiday saver: a family keeping a holiday tradition alive over 35 years, with long gaps when children, work or money made travel impossible, checking whether a fund kept for decades still matches the trip the family wants now.

FAQs

What is a good ROI?
There is no single good figure. Compare returns over the same period and with the same risk before deciding.

Is this holiday calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the annual rate fall to 1.17% at 35 years even though total return is unchanged?
Because the same $5,000 gain is spread over 35 years instead of 5. As an illustration only, the 35th root of 1.50 is about 1.0117, so the yearly rate is about 1.17%, even though the total is still 50.00%.

How do long gaps without a holiday affect a 35-year reading?
They do not change the maths, but they change the meaning. A fund untouched for years may look patient, yet the trip it was meant to pay for may have become far more expensive in the meantime.

Should a 35-year holiday return be compared with a shorter, safer reserve?
Yes. Compare it with what a shorter-term holiday reserve would have done over the same years, and with the current cost of the trip. A very long horizon alone is not a reason to accept a very slow yearly rate.
