Education Monthly Budget Split Calculator (15 Years)

Split monthly income into needs, wants and savings. This education version uses a 15-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Needs are 50 percent, wants are 30 percent and savings are 20 percent of income.

How it works

This 50-30-20 split is a planning guide, not a rule. Adjust it to your real costs.

Worked example

Monthly income $4,000, term 15 years. A household earns $4,000 a month and is looking 15 years ahead to a child's university years. Using the page formula, needs are 50 percent of $4,000, which is $2,000 a month, wants are 30 percent, which is $1,200 a month, and savings are 20 percent, which is $800 a month, adding back to $4,000 in total. The term in years does not change this monthly answer, because the formula uses only monthly income. As a multi-year extension only, not a forecast, assuming the same $4,000 income and the same split repeat every month with no pay rise, fee rise or inflation counted, the totals over 15 years are $360,000 for needs, $216,000 for wants and $144,000 for savings, over 180 months. At 15 years the warning matters as much as the total: $144,000 of savings looks comfortable, but 15 years of fee rises, course changes and family changes can easily move the real target, so this figure should be re-checked, not locked in.

Who this term suits for an education planner: parents of a young child who want a long, steady education fund, putting $800 a month aside for 15 years toward a degree, while keeping wants at $1,200 so the plan survives school holidays, trips and rising household costs.

FAQs

What is the 50-30-20 rule?
It is a simple budget guide: 50 percent needs, 30 percent wants and 20 percent savings.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is 15 years the sweet spot for education saving?
It is a practical middle horizon: long enough for $144,000 of savings to build at $800 a month, but short enough that a family can still picture the child, the course and the university. Shorter plans need higher monthly savings; longer plans need more guesswork about fees.

What happens if fees rise faster than income over 15 years?
Then the $144,000 target falls short, because this extension counts no fee rise at all. A family should add a fee-rise allowance each year, or raise the $800 saving, rather than trusting a 15-year-old estimate.

Can a 15-year fund cover a second degree or postgraduate study?
Only if the first degree costs less than $144,000 or the family keeps saving after the first degree. Planning for two qualifications on one $800-a-month fund usually needs a higher monthly amount or a longer horizon.
