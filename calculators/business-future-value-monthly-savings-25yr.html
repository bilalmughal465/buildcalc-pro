Business Future Value Monthly Savings Calculator (25 Years)

Estimate the future value of regular monthly savings. This business version uses a 25-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The Future value result shows the total amount the regular monthly deposits are worth at the end of the term, including interest earned along the way, so it can be compared with the savings goal for the business.

Formula

Future value uses the monthly rate and the number of monthly deposits to grow each deposit with interest.

How it works

Each monthly deposit earns interest from the month it is made until the end of the term.

Worked example

A business sets aside $500 every month for 25 years at 6% a year. The monthly rate is 0.5%, and there are 300 monthly deposits. Using the page formula, the future value is $500 times ((1.005 to the power of 300 minus 1) divided by 0.005), which is $346,496.98. The business paid in $150,000, so interest added $196,496.98, which is 56.7% of the final value. Past 25 years, interest overtakes the deposits by a wide margin and becomes the main source of growth.

Who this term suits for a business: A multi-site plan: a firm funding a second and third location from its own reserve, or a family business building a fund that the next generation can use to expand rather than to repay inherited debt.

FAQs

How does monthly saving grow?
Regular deposits grow because each deposit earns interest, and that interest then earns more interest.

Is this business calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why is interest now larger than all the deposits combined?
Because the balance has grown large enough that 0.5% a month on the balance adds more than the $500 deposit. From here on, protecting the balance matters as much as adding to it.

Should deposits rise with profits over 25 years?
Yes, if the goal grows too. A flat $500 deposit for 25 years is simple to model, but a business whose profits tripled should ask whether the goal, and the deposit, should have grown with it.

How does a business downturn affect a 25-year fund?
A pause costs compounding, but raiding the fund costs more. The fund should be sized so a bad year means a smaller deposit, not an empty reserve.
