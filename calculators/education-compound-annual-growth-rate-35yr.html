Education Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (35 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This education version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 35 years. First, the growth ratio is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00. Using the page formula, CAGR is 2.00 to the power of one over 35, minus one, which is 2.00% a year. That is the steady yearly rate that would take $10,000 to $20,000 in 35 years, if growth were perfectly smooth every year.

At 35 years the rate rounds to 2.00% a year. The doubling is real, but the pace is now so gentle that a single year of fee rises could outrun it, which is why long trusts are reviewed against costs, not against the doubling. Remember that CAGR smooths the path: the fund may have grown unevenly, with flat or negative years in between, and still show 2.00% a year. It describes the past window, it does not promise the next 35 years.

Who this term suits for an education saver: a multi-decade education trust set up for grandchildren, measured over 35 years, where the trustees need to explain to the family why a fund that doubled shows a yearly rate of only about 2.00%.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this education calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is 2.00% a year over 35 years enough for education costs that rise faster?
No, not if costs rose faster than 2.00% a year on average. The fund doubled in cash, but if fees more than doubled over 35 years, it buys less education than it did at the start.

Why does the rate fall so slowly after 30 years?
Because the maths flattens: going from 30 to 35 years spreads the same doubling over five more years, but the rate is already low, so each extra year removes only a small slice.

Should a 35-year education fund be topped up?
Only if the remaining gap demands it. Compare the fund with the fee bill it must meet, in today's prices, rather than topping up simply because the yearly rate looks low.
