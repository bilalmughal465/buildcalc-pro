Home Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (20 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This home version uses a 20-year planning term as its starting point.

—

The result labelled CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take the starting value to the ending value if growth were perfectly even every year, so a CAGR of 3.53% over 20 years describes a smooth average pace, not a rise that happened by that amount in any single year.

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 20 years. Using the page formula, CAGR equals $20,000 divided by $10,000, to the power of one over 20, minus one, which is 3.53% a year. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the steady yearly rate that would grow $10,000 to $20,000 over 20 years if growth were perfectly even every year.

Note that 3.53% is lower than the simple average of 5.00% a year, which just divides the 100% total gain by 20 years. The simple average ignores compounding, so it overstates the steady rate; the CAGR figure is the one that actually reproduces the doubling when compounded year after year.

Who this term suits for a home household: A home household looking back over a full mortgage-era stretch, for example owners who bought about 20 years ago and want the single yearly rate that explains how their home value doubled.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this home calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Is 3.53% a year low for a home over 20 years?
Not necessarily. Over 20 years, 3.53% a year still doubles the value, and it is a realistic long-run pace once inflation and market cycles are considered.

How can such a small rate double the value?
Through compounding: 3.53% added every year for 20 years multiplies $10,000 by two, even though no single year looks dramatic.

What home costs does this 20-year rate leave out?
Mortgage interest, maintenance, repairs, insurance, taxes and selling fees, so the owner's real profit is less than the headline doubling.
