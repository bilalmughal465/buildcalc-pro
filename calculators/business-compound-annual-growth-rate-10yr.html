Business Compound Annual Growth Rate Calculator (10 Years)

Calculate compound annual growth over a period. This business version uses a 10-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

CAGR equals ending value divided by starting value, to the power of one over years, minus one.

How it works

CAGR smooths growth into a single yearly rate, even if actual yearly growth varied.

Worked example

Starting value $10,000, ending value $20,000, term 10 years. The ratio is $20,000 divided by $10,000, which is 2.00. Raise 2.00 to the power of one over 10, subtract one, and multiply by 100: the compound annual growth rate is 7.18%. That means the business value would need to grow by about 7.18% every single year, steadily, to double in 10 years.

Over 10 years the same doubling reads as 7.18% a year, which is solid rather than spectacular: it suits an established business that grew through good and bad years, and it is the kind of track record a bank or investor takes seriously. These figures are nominal, before inflation, fees, and tax, and actual yearly growth will have varied; CAGR smooths it into one rate.

Who this term suits for a business: an established small or medium business preparing a loan application or investor pitch: a 10-year CAGR of 7.18% shows durable growth, and pairing it with profit figures makes the pitch far stronger than a single good year.

FAQs

What does CAGR mean?
CAGR is the steady yearly growth rate that would take a starting value to an ending value.

Is this business calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the same doubling look smaller over 10 years than over 5 years?
Because the growth is spread over twice as many years. Doubling in 10 years needs only about 7.18% a year, while doubling in 5 years needs about 14.87% a year, even though the total growth is 100% in both cases.

Is 7.18% a year enough to beat rising costs over 10 years?
Only if costs rose more slowly than 7.18% a year. If rent, wages, and supplies rose faster, the business may have grown in revenue but shrunk in real profit, so check margins alongside CAGR.

How should I compare a 10-year CAGR with a competitor's 5-year CAGR?
Do not compare them directly. Restate both over the same period, or compare each business with its own earlier period, otherwise the shorter, faster-looking rate will always win unfairly.
