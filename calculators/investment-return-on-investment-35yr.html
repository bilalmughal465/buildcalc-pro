Investment Return on Investment Calculator (35 Years)

Calculate return on investment as a percentage. This investment version uses a 35-year planning term as its starting point.

—

Formula

Return equals final value minus invested amount, divided by invested amount, times 100.

How it works

A positive return means the final value is higher than the amount invested.

Worked example

Amount invested $10,000, final value $15,000, term 35 years. Using the page formula, return equals $15,000 minus $10,000, divided by $10,000, times 100, which is $5,000 divided by $10,000, times 100, which is 50.00%. That is the calculator's own result for these defaults, and it is the same on every page in this group because the term is not an input.

That 50.00% is a total over the whole term, not a yearly rate. As illustrations only, not outputs of this page, the simple average is 50 divided by 35, which is 1.43% a year, and the steady rate that would compound $10,000 to $15,000 over 35 years is 1.17% a year. Over 35 years, the headline total says almost nothing about the yearly result.

Who this term suits for an investor: an investor auditing a multi-decade holding, for example a trust, a family business stake, or a retirement fund nearing payout, where the honest question is the yearly rate after decades of fees and inflation.

FAQs

What is a good ROI?
There is no single good figure. Compare returns over the same period and with the same risk before deciding.

Is this investment calculator exact?
No, it is an estimate for planning only, based on the numbers you enter.

Are these figures financial advice?
No, they are estimates for planning only, not financial advice. Check with a qualified adviser for decisions involving large sums.

Why does the yearly figure matter more than the 35-year total?
Because a 50% total over 35 years is only 1.17% a year compounded. Totals flatter long holdings, while yearly rates expose whether the money worked hard or just sat still.

Who keeps a 35-year investment return if the original investor retires?
Whoever inherits or takes over the holding. A 35-year figure spans working lives, so the next holder should rejudge the investment on its own merits, not on its history.

Is 35 years too long to judge from one invested amount and one final value?
Yes, on its own. One start and one end point hide every crash, fee rise, and income payment in between. Split the period before trusting the figure.
